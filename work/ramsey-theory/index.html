01 / What I wrote

Two chapters.
One mathematical thread.

The chapters turn geometric and Ramsey questions into set-intersection problems, then use linear algebra and the polynomial method to prove bounds.

08

Chapter 8 · Manuscript pages 2–5

How many colors does space need?

I wrote the chapter on the chromatic number of Euclidean space: the minimum number of colors needed so that points one unit apart receive different colors.

  • Developed the exposition from simplex and spindle constructions to quadratic, polynomial, and exponential lower bounds.
  • Worked through the Frankl–Wilson set-intersection construction and the prime-parameter optimization giving exponential rate β = (1 + √2)/2 ≈ 1.2071.
  • Placed this construction alongside stronger bounds from the literature, making the method’s reach clear.
10

Chapter 10 · Manuscript pages 6–11

From existence to an explicit construction.

I wrote the chapter on constructive Ramsey lower bounds: building edge colorings that avoid large monochromatic cliques, rather than only proving that such colorings exist.

  • Connected Oddtown and Fisher’s inequality to cubic constructions, then developed the Frankl–Wilson construction beyond every fixed polynomial degree.
  • Included the parameter optimization behind m = p³ and the interpolation argument that extends the bound to every sufficiently large k.
  • Compared explicit constructions with probabilistic bounds and explained the later connection to dispersers and extractors.

02 / The common idea

Encode the problem.
Control the intersections.

01

Represent

Use finite sets as vertices, or embed their incidence vectors as points in space.

02

Constrain

Translate a shared edge color or a forbidden unit distance into an intersection condition.

03

Bound

Use polynomial evaluation and rank to limit how large a color class or homogeneous family can be.

These chapters explain established results and their proofs, including the intersection methods of Frankl and Wilson. The contribution is the mathematical exposition: building the argument, filling in its steps, and connecting the ideas.

03 / Extending the slides

Making the proof
easier to follow.

I also added material to the Frankl–Wilson slide deck, credited jointly to William Gasarch and me.

The expanded deck moves from Oddtown and Fisher’s inequality to polynomial methods and their Ramsey and Euclidean applications. It includes worked examples, an exponential Euclidean bound using primes alone, and the interpolation needed for the constructive Ramsey bound.

Later slides discuss corrections to the Ramsey writeup and explain why the prime-power case needs a separate argument. The deck credits earlier exposition by Danesh Sivakumar and slides by Kelin Zhu.
